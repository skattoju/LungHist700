\section{Introduction and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:intro}

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide~\cite{sung2021}, with accurate histopathological subtype identification being critical for treatment planning. Manual tissue slide analysis is time-consuming, subject to inter-pathologist variability, and impractical at scale. Automated deep learning approaches can improve consistency and throughput, but face a unique set of challenges compared to natural-image recognition tasks.

\paragraph{Challenges in the Literature.}
The primary difficulties in computational lung histopathology are: (i)~\textbf{Limited labelled data} — unlike internet-scale image datasets, annotated histopathology collections are expensive to produce and typically contain only a few hundred to a few thousand slides. This constrains model capacity and makes overfitting a persistent concern~\cite{srinidhi2021}. (ii)~\textbf{Patient-level data leakage} — patches extracted from the same patient are highly correlated; splitting the dataset at the patch level rather than the patient level leads to inflated metrics that do not generalise to unseen patients~\cite{tellez2019}. (iii)~\textbf{Weak supervision and the MIL problem} — clinical labels annotate entire slides (or images), not individual regions of interest. Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) addresses this by treating each slide as an unordered \emph{bag} of patch instances and learning only from bag-level labels, but introduces a significantly harder optimisation problem than patch-level supervision~\cite{ilse2018,campanella2019}. (iv)~\textbf{ImageNet domain gap} — convolutional backbones pretrained on natural images do not fully capture H\&E stain morphology, nuclear texture, or tissue architecture~\cite{kather2019,chen2022}. (v)~\textbf{Class imbalance} — datasets often oversample common subtypes, biasing models toward majority classes. Common mitigations include patient-wise splitting, class-weighted loss, transfer learning from ImageNet-pretrained or histopathology-specific encoders, regularisation, and attention-based MIL aggregators.

\paragraph{Project Goals and Expected Results.}
Our project evaluates four approaches on LungHist700~\cite{lunghist700paper} (691 high-resolution histopathological lung images, 20$\times$/40$\times$ magnification) in a three-class setting: adenocarcinoma (ACA), normal (NOR), and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). We aim to (i)~reproduce the ResNet50 patch-level CNN baseline and verify it against published benchmarks, (ii)~implement an end-to-end MIL pipeline following the paper's bag construction, (iii)~implement frozen-backbone MIL with multi-head self-attention and gated attention aggregators as a computationally efficient alternative, and (iv)~report accuracy, macro-F1, and ROC-AUC complemented by confusion matrices, learning curves, and qualitative attention visualizations. Based on the literature, we expect the patch-level CNN to outperform MIL in raw accuracy owing to denser supervision, while MIL is expected to provide better generalisation under weak labels, and frozen-backbone MIL with gated attention to approach end-to-end training at far lower computational cost.

\section{Proposed Methodologies}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Dataset and Experimental Setup}
LungHist700~\cite{lunghist700paper} comprises 691 high-resolution (1200$\times$1600~px) histopathological lung images at 20$\times$ and 40$\times$ magnification, spanning seven fine-grained sub-classes grouped into three diagnostic superclasses: adenocarcinoma (ACA), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and normal tissue (NOR). All experiments use the 20$\times$ subset. To prevent patient-level data leakage, we apply strict patient-wise splitting: patient IDs are partitioned into non-overlapping train, validation, and test sets. Class-weighted cross-entropy loss is used throughout to mitigate the moderate class imbalance.

\subsection{Baseline CNN (ResNet50 Transfer Learning)}
Our supervised baseline fine-tunes a ResNet50 backbone~\cite{resnet} pretrained on ImageNet-1K for 3-way classification using weighted cross-entropy loss with Adam optimiser (lr $=$ 1e-4) and \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} scheduling. Images are resized to 300$\times$400~px and randomly cropped to 224$\times$224~px patches at training time. Crucially, LungHist700 provides only \emph{image-level labels} — no per-patch or region-level annotations exist. The CNN baseline therefore propagates the image-level label to \emph{every patch} extracted from that image, treating each crop as a fully-supervised training example. This is a common but noisy assumption: patches largely containing stroma or background inherit the same label as patches containing the diagnostic region. However, because the label is applied once per patch (many times per image), the model receives far more gradient updates per image than MIL, which is the source of its convergence advantage. Training augmentations include horizontal and vertical flips, random $\gamma$, brightness/contrast jitter, and HSV colour shifts. Grad-CAM~\cite{gradcam} saliency maps are used to verify spatial focus (see Supplement~A).

\subsection{End-to-End MIL (ResNet50 + Multi-Head Self-Attention)}
The end-to-end MIL model treats each slide as a \emph{bag} of $N=20$ randomly sampled 224$\times$224~px patches extracted from the full-resolution 1200$\times$1600~px image — matching the bag construction described in the original LungHist700 paper~\cite{lunghist700paper}. A shared ResNet50 backbone encodes each patch independently into a 2048-dimensional embedding. A 4-head self-attention module then computes weighted inter-patch relationships, and the resulting representation is average-pooled and passed to a linear classifier. The entire model — backbone and attention head — is trained jointly under bag-level weak supervision. This formulation allows the network to jointly learn which patches are discriminative and how to extract discriminative features from them, but creates a chicken-and-egg optimisation problem known as the \emph{cold-start} issue. Attention weight visualizations are provided in Supplement~C.

\subsection{Frozen-Backbone MIL (Offline Embeddings + Lightweight Aggregators)}
To address training instability and computational cost, we decouple feature extraction from aggregation~\cite{chen2022}: the ImageNet ResNet50 is frozen and used offline to pre-compute 2048-dimensional embeddings for 35 deterministic grid patches per image (5$\times$7 grid, full coverage of the 1200$\times$1600 image). These fixed embeddings are saved to disk and reused across training runs, reducing wall-clock training time drastically. Two lightweight aggregation heads are then trained exclusively on the fixed embeddings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multi-Head Self-Attention MIL:} 4-head self-attention over the 35 patch embeddings, followed by mean-pooling across patches and a linear classifier. Self-attention models pairwise inter-patch relationships, potentially capturing spatial co-occurrence patterns.
    \item \textbf{Gated Attention MIL~\cite{ilse2018}:} per-patch importance scores computed via sigmoid-tanh gating, followed by a weighted-sum aggregation and a dropout-regularised linear classifier. Gating produces sparse, interpretable patch weights and scales linearly with bag size, in contrast to the quadratic cost of self-attention.
\end{itemize}
Training curves for both aggregators appear in Supplement~B.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
All models are evaluated on the same held-out patient-wise test split. We report accuracy, Macro Precision, Macro Recall, Macro-F1, and ROC-AUC. Normalized confusion matrices (per-class recall) identify which class pairs are most frequently confused. Epoch-wise learning curves (loss, accuracy, AUROC) diagnose convergence behaviour and potential overfitting on the small dataset.

\section{Attempts at Solving the Problem}
\label{sec:attempts}

\subsection{Environment Setup and Implementation Challenges}
Establishing a stable training environment required resolving CUDA/cuDNN library visibility under WSL2, TensorFlow--protobuf version alignment, and missing default dataset path assumptions in the baseline scripts. A critical MIL-specific bug was also identified and corrected: patches were being extracted from a downscaled 300$\times$400~px version of the image rather than the full 1200$\times$1600~px original. This discarded the micro-texture detail — nuclear size, gland architecture, keratinisation patterns — that constitutes the primary discriminative signal for the attention mechanism. After correction, the model extracts 224$\times$224~px patches directly from the high-resolution source, preserving all clinically relevant detail.

\subsection{Results and Analysis}

\paragraph{CNN Baseline.}
The fine-tuned ResNet50 achieves \textbf{Acc 91.7\%, AUROC 99.5\%, Macro-F1 0.915} (Tab.~\ref{tab:all_results}), closely reproducing the published LungHist700 benchmark of $\approx$90\% accuracy and 98\% AUROC. The CNN's advantage over MIL is not a result of having richer labels — both approaches use the same image-level annotations — but rather arises from treating each randomly cropped patch as an independent training example with the propagated image label. This produces many labelled gradient steps per image, enabling fast, stable convergence. The trade-off is label noise: patches predominantly containing stroma from an ACA image are incorrectly labelled as ACA. Despite this noise, empirical performance is high: NOR is classified near-perfectly and the main error is ACA/SCC confusion due to overlapping morphology. Grad-CAM maps (Supplement~A) confirm focus on glandular and nuclear structures. Training converges within $\approx$30 epochs without severe overfitting.

\paragraph{End-to-End MIL Baseline.}
The jointly trained backbone $+$ self-attention model reaches \textbf{Acc 70.8\%, AUROC 91.3\%, Macro-F1 0.701}. The $\approx$20-point accuracy gap relative to the CNN arises directly from the weaker supervision regime: a single bag-level label must guide the gradient through $\approx$25~million ResNet50 parameters, resulting in noisy, slow convergence. Furthermore, the attention module must learn which patches are discriminative before the backbone features are stable — the cold-start problem identified by~\cite{srinidhi2021}. Our accuracy falls $\approx$10 points below the paper's reported MIL figure (81\%); however, \textbf{AUROC 91.3\% exceeds the paper's value of 89\%}, indicating well-calibrated class probability estimates. This suggests the model is competitive for risk-stratification applications where ranking is more important than hard classification. Attention maps (Supplement~C) confirm that the model learns to assign high weights to patches containing discriminative morphological features.

\paragraph{Frozen-Backbone MIL.}
Both frozen aggregators match or approach the end-to-end MIL accuracy while eliminating backbone gradient computation entirely (Tab.~\ref{tab:all_results}). \textbf{Gated attention} (Acc 70.8\%, AUROC 86.6\%, F1 0.700) outperforms self-attention on every metric. Its sparse, per-patch independent scoring is more amenable to regularisation in this small-bag, small-dataset setting: NOR precision reaches 93\% and ACA recall 83\%. \textbf{Multi-head self-attention} (Acc 68.75\%, AUROC 86.1\%, F1 0.647) underperforms because its higher parameter count leads to overfitting — SCC recall collapses to 36\%, the worst value across all models. SCC is consistently the most challenging class across all MIL variants (recall 36--55\%), indicating that ImageNet ResNet50 features are insufficiently specialised for squamous-cell morphology. AUROC for both frozen models ($\approx$0.86) is lower than the end-to-end MIL (0.913), confirming that joint backbone training — despite its instability — still produces more histology-adapted features.

\paragraph{Discussion.}
Our experiments confirm the CNN $>$ MIL accuracy ordering reported in~\cite{lunghist700paper}. Importantly, both approaches use the same image-level labels — the CNN does not have access to richer annotations. The CNN's empirical advantage stems from propagating the image-level label to every patch, generating many gradient updates per image at the cost of label noise (non-diagnostic patches inherit the slide diagnosis). MIL is the more \emph{principled} formulation — it correctly treats the image-level label as a bag label without making this noisy per-patch assumption — but requires the model to simultaneously learn which patches matter and how to represent them, making optimisation significantly harder. Among MIL variants, gated attention is the most practical choice for small datasets, offering lower parameter count, linear complexity in bag size, and interpretable per-patch importance scores. The frozen-backbone setup demonstrates competitive performance without any backbone gradient computation. The persistent SCC challenge across all MIL models points to the ImageNet domain gap: backbones trained on natural images do not capture H\&E stain morphology, keratinisation, or squamous-cell architecture, and domain-adapted histopathology encoders~\cite{chen2022,kather2019} are expected to close this gap significantly.

\section{Future Steps}
\label{sec:future}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Domain-adapted backbone:} Replace ImageNet ResNet50 with a histopathology-specific encoder (UNI~\cite{chen2022}, CONCH, or PLIP) to close the feature domain gap, particularly for SCC classification.
\item \textbf{Vision Transformer (ViT):} Evaluate ViT (default ImageNet and fine-tuned variants) as an alternative backbone for both patch-level and frozen-MIL pipelines. Hierarchical ViT architectures such as HIPT~\cite{chen2022} are particularly promising for capturing multi-scale spatial context.
\item \textbf{Qualitative interpretability:} Extend Grad-CAM (CNN) and attention weight heatmaps (MIL) to larger sample sets, and validate alignment with pathologist-defined regions of interest.
\item \textbf{Statistical validation:} Report mean $\pm$ std over multiple random seeds to quantify result variance in the small-data regime and establish confidence intervals for cross-model comparisons.
\item \textbf{40$\times$ resolution experiments:} Replicate the full pipeline at 40$\times$ magnification to assess the sensitivity of each approach to imaging resolution, as higher magnification captures finer nuclear detail that may benefit SCC recognition.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{E\quad Data Augmentation Details}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\noindent The original LungHist700 paper~\cite{lunghist700paper} states only that augmentations were ``dynamically generated using the Albumentations library'' during training, without enumerating the specific transforms. Our pipeline is more specifically documented below.

\paragraph{Augmentation strategy overview.}
Three distinct augmentation configurations are used across pipelines:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{CNN / End-to-End MIL (online, per-epoch):} augmentations are applied to each patch on-the-fly during every training step, so the model sees a different crop and colour variation at every epoch.
    \item \textbf{Frozen MIL embedding extraction (offline, pre-computed):} because embeddings are cached to disk, augmentation cannot be applied on-the-fly. Instead, \textbf{5 augmented copies} of each training image's patches are pre-generated and saved as separate \texttt{.pt} files. During training, the dataset randomly selects one of these copies per sample per epoch.
    \item \textbf{Validation / Test (all pipelines):} no spatial or colour augmentation; only ImageNet normalisation is applied to ensure deterministic evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Notes:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item CNN downscales to 300$\times$400 before random cropping; MIL pipelines crop directly from the full 1200$\times$1600 image to preserve micro-texture detail.
